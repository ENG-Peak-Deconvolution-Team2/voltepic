Explainability · ENG work package 5

Where the model looks, and how we checked.

An explanation is a claim about the model, so we tested it like one. Four methods produced maps of where the detector looks; five tests asked whether those maps are faithful, stable, on the peak, free of artefacts, and tied to what the model learned. The analyzer shows the methods that held up.

Two questions

Is the explanation true to the model, or true to chemistry?

They sound alike and are often mixed up. A map can match chemistry and still misdescribe the model, or describe the model faithfully and contradict chemistry.

Question A · asked here

Why did the model decide this?

Faithfulness and stability: does the map reflect what the model actually uses, and does it stay the same when the model is retrained? This is what work package 5 measures.

Question B · read alongside

Does the evidence agree with what we know?

Plausibility: does the map point at the analyte's known peak? We have that outside reference for PVDF — single-analyte peak windows — so the E3 test reads the maps against it. A match is a hint, not a proof of chemistry.

    The edges

    Why some maps light up the ends.

    A six-level wavelet transform on 200 points reaches the ends of the scan. Some of its coefficients see almost nothing but the first or last ten points — and the model does use them. So part of the weight that maps put on the edges is real.

    The other part comes from the fill. Bridging a masked window with a straight line (R3) is harmless in the middle of a peak but, at the edge, it has only one neighbour and flattens the start of the scan; in the tests that fill pushed the edge share to 35% and was flagged.
